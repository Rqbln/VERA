\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Deploying large language models under the EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact2024} requires reproducible technical evidence across trustworthy-AI principles~\cite{hleg2019trustworthyai}.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} decomposes these obligations into eighteen technical requirements, twelve of which admit measurable scores, but industrial practice still relies on fragmented benchmark scripts whose aggregation choices are opaque and whose results are hard for non-specialists to triage.
We present RAIP, an open-source, self-hostable software-engineering platform \emph{and} a ``compliance control room'' dashboard for EU AI Act LLM evaluation.
RAIP orchestrates benchmarks through a LangGraph pipeline (CLI, REST API, Celery workers), routes inference via LiteLLM to self-hosted models, and emits signed artifacts with explicit harness provenance.
Two design decisions distinguish it.
First, the benchmark-to-requirement \emph{aggregation weights} are a first-class, versioned, signed artifact; we use this to run a reproducible \emph{sensitivity analysis} that quantifies how the per-requirement compliance picture, the green/amber/red bands, and the dashboard's status-first triage order shift under alternative weightings.
Second, a no-login \emph{guided} mode lets a non-technical user launch an evaluation on a connected model and read a status-first, progressively-disclosed summary, while a role-based control room serves expert audiences.
On an open-weight target we report measurable-requirement scores with bootstrap intervals, the weighting-sensitivity result, and an expert interpretability walkthrough of the dashboard.
RAIP treats compliance-oriented evaluation as an automated, reproducible, and \emph{auditable} software pipeline rather than ad hoc spreadsheet assembly.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
Regulated and high-risk AI systems must demonstrate technical evidence before deployment.
The EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact2024} and the Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI~\cite{hleg2019trustworthyai} frame expectations across robustness, privacy, transparency, and fairness.
Organizational frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework~\cite{nist2023airmf} and ISO/IEC~42001~\cite{iso42001_2023} guide governance but do not provide an executable pipeline that runs LLM benchmarks, aggregates scores, and produces auditor-facing artifacts.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} translates legal principles into eighteen technical requirements with associated benchmarks, yet remains a measurement framework rather than an orchestration platform.
Individual suites---for example DecodingTrust~\cite{wang2023decodingtrust}, the language-model evaluation harness~\cite{gao2021lmevalharness}, and MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}---answer \emph{measurement} questions in isolation.
They do not, by themselves, deliver orchestration, requirement-level aggregation, or governance artifacts such as model cards~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard} and datasheets for datasets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets}.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
Three gaps motivate our work.
First, compliance and ML teams manually assemble scores from disconnected tools, which is error-prone and difficult to reproduce across release gates.
Second, inference-only evaluation misses dataset-stage risks (COMPL-AI CR03--CR05) and training-time persistence effects such as backdoor survival after alignment.
Third, when optional dependencies are absent, evaluations may silently degrade to heuristic scorers without recording which harness actually ran.
Consider a compliance engineer who must attach a model card showing robustness (CR01), fairness and toxicity (CR10--CR12), and signed run metadata before approving an instruction-tuned LLM, and who must also justify \emph{how} the per-requirement scores were aggregated---a process that today often requires days of ad hoc scripting and undocumented weighting choices.

\subsection{Platform Requirements}
We distinguish \emph{platform requirements} PR1--PR4, which an evaluation platform must satisfy, from COMPL-AI \emph{requirement identifiers} CR01--CR12, which label measurable risk dimensions.

\subsection{Contributions}
We present RAIP (\emph{Responsible AI in Practice}), an open-source platform that addresses PR1--PR4 while operationalizing COMPL-AI measurable requirements.
Unlike prior LLM trustworthiness tools that focus primarily on single-shot model prompts without longitudinal or data-stage coverage~\cite{sunetnanta2025promptops}, RAIP unifies benchmark dispatch, weighted aggregation, and governance artifact generation in one self-hostable pipeline.

In this paper, we make the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1 --- Open platform and compliance control room:}
  An open-source, self-hostable implementation (CLI \texttt{raip-eval}, REST API, Celery workers, LangGraph supervisor, benchmark registry with dedicated runners) \emph{and} a Next.js ``control room'' dashboard with status-first triage, progressive disclosure, role-based lenses, and a no-login \emph{guided} mode whose launch wizard lets a non-technical user evaluate a connected model in minutes.
  \item \textbf{C2 --- Configurable, auditable aggregation:}
  Benchmark-to-requirement aggregation weights as a first-class, versioned, signed artifact (\texttt{benchmarks\_catalog.yaml}), and a reproducible \emph{weighting-sensitivity methodology} that quantifies how requirement scores, score bands, and the dashboard's triage order move under alternative weightings.
  \item \textbf{C3 --- Empirical evaluation:}
  On an open-weight target, (RQ1) reproducible COMPL-AI-aligned scores with bootstrap intervals and harness provenance, (RQ2) a weighting-sensitivity study reporting band-flips and triage-rank stability, and (RQ3) an expert interpretability walkthrough of the control room.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Regulatory Framing and COMPL-AI}
The EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact2024} and the six HLEG trustworthy-AI principles~\cite{hleg2019trustworthyai} motivate technical evidence for high-risk systems.
NIST and ISO frameworks~\cite{nist2023airmf,iso42001_2023} guide organizational risk management; RAIP implements the \emph{measure} slice---automated benchmarks and artifacts---not full governance programs.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} maps legal principles to eighteen technical requirements, twelve with measurable scores in $[0,1]$, and associates benchmarks and multi-model studies.
COMPL-AI remains a benchmark \emph{framework}, not a lifecycle orchestration platform with signed audit chains.

\subsection{Benchmark Suites and Metrics}
RAIP orchestrates established suites rather than proposing new benchmark science.
Capability probes (CR06) draw on MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}, GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, HumanEval~\cite{chen2021humaneval}, TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa}, and BBH~\cite{suzgun2023bbh} via the language-model evaluation harness~\cite{gao2021lmevalharness}.
Calibration (CR07) uses expected calibration error~\cite{guo2017calibration}.
Fairness and bias (CR10--CR11) integrate BBQ~\cite{parrish2022bbq}, BOLD~\cite{dhamala2021bold}, StereoSet~\cite{nadeem2021stereoset}, and disparity metrics~\cite{hardt2016equality,wang2023decodingtrust}.
Toxicity (CR12) uses RealToxicityPrompts~\cite{gehman2020realtoxicity}.
Dataset-stage checks (CR03--CR05) apply corpus heuristics and optional libraries; membership and extraction attacks~\cite{carlini2021membership,carlini2023extracting} motivate simplified engineering checks in MVP2.
Watermarking (CR09) follows a statistical heuristic inspired by~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}, not production SynthID.
Cyber probes (CR02) may invoke Garak-class tools in implementation (no bibliography entry).

\subsection{Governance Artifacts}
Model Cards~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard} and Datasheets for Datasets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets} standardize disclosure; RAIP emits both from evaluation runs.

\subsection{Open-Source LLM Evaluation Frameworks}
A growing ecosystem of open-source frameworks evaluates LLMs, but they target different layers.
HELM~\cite{liang2023helm} standardizes \emph{which} scenarios and metrics to report across many models; the language-model evaluation harness~\cite{gao2021lmevalharness} standardizes \emph{how} a capability benchmark is executed reproducibly; Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} is a security-probing framework for jailbreaks and prompt injection; DecodingTrust~\cite{wang2023decodingtrust} is a trustworthiness benchmark suite.
These are excellent \emph{measurement} substrates, and RAIP orchestrates them rather than competing with them.
What turns such tools into a usable \emph{compliance framework}---rather than a collection of scripts---is, we argue, four engineering properties: (i) a permissive open license and self-hostable deployment; (ii) extensibility through a benchmark registry and pluggable runners; (iii) reproducibility via declarative runs, versioned and signed catalogues, and recorded provenance; and (iv) an interpretable restitution surface that maps raw metrics to regulatory requirement identifiers for non-specialist stakeholders.
RAIP's contribution is to assemble these four properties around the COMPL-AI requirement taxonomy and to make the \emph{aggregation policy} itself an auditable artifact.

\subsection{Gap Summary}
No reference in \Cref{tab:related-work} combines a COMPL-AI-complete requirement mapping~\cite{guldimann2024complai}, a declarative self-hostable pipeline, \emph{configurable and signed} aggregation weights, recorded harness provenance, and an interpretive compliance dashboard in one platform---which is precisely the gap RAIP targets.

\section{RAIP Framework and Architecture}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Conceptual Evaluation Model}
An \emph{evaluation target} (a model endpoint, a training checkpoint, or a dataset corpus) is tagged with a \emph{lifecycle stage} (\texttt{data}, \texttt{finetune}, \texttt{inference}, \ldots) in a declarative \texttt{benchmark\_run.yaml}.
The COMPL-AI layer aggregates the twelve measurable identifiers CR01--CR12 from weighted benchmarks in the signed catalogue \texttt{mvp2-v1} (\Cref{sec:weighting}), with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals.
The artifact layer emits \texttt{benchmark\_run.yaml}, \texttt{model\_card.md}, \texttt{datasheet.md} for dataset runs, and \texttt{raw\_outputs.jsonl} with per-benchmark harness metadata.
Compliance stakeholders consume requirement tables and signatures, engineers consume MLflow traces, and the control room (\Cref{sec:dashboard}) renders all of it for a release-gate audience.

\subsection{System Architecture}
\Cref{fig:architecture} shows the major components.
Users invoke \texttt{raip-eval}, \texttt{POST /api/v1/runs}, or the guided launch wizard; Celery workers execute a LangGraph supervisor (\texttt{evaluate} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{aggregate}); LiteLLM routes inference to the target (default Ollama) and to a self-hosted judge; artifacts persist to MinIO or, in the lite profile, the local filesystem, with MLflow tracking optional.

\subsection{Benchmark Orchestration and Provenance}
Signed weights in \texttt{benchmarks\_catalog.yaml} (version \texttt{mvp2-v1}) define per-requirement contributions.
Each \texttt{raw\_outputs} row records \texttt{harness}, \texttt{agent}, and optional \texttt{fallback: true} with \texttt{fallback\_reason} when Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} or \texttt{lm\_eval} are unavailable, so a reviewer can always see which scorer actually produced a number.

\subsection{Lifecycle Lab Extensions}
Dataset scans populate CR03--CR05 in-graph: toxicity via Detoxify~\cite{hanu2020detoxify}, copyright via memorisation heuristics, and privacy via Presidio~\cite{microsoft2023presidio} PII detection.
Checkpoint evaluation reuses the graph and writes Timescale \texttt{metric\_timeseries}.
A poisoning lab reports backdoor survival ratio for CR02 extensions, motivated by deceptive-agent persistence~\cite{hubinger2024sleeperagents}.
These lifecycle extensions exist in the platform but are not exercised in this paper's evaluation, which centres on inference and the dashboard.

\subsection{Governance Artifact Generation}
Jinja2 model cards follow Model Cards~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard}: twelve measurable rows, dataset evaluation URIs, harness provenance, signatures, and limitations.
Datasheets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets} attach to corpus scans.
Environmental hooks align with ML CO$_2$ methodology~\cite{henderson2020mlco2}.

\subsection{Implementation}
The \texttt{raip} package targets Python~3.11 and ships two deployment profiles: a single-command \emph{lite} stack (Redis + API + worker + Next.js dashboard, local-filesystem artifacts, no login) for non-technical users, and a full Docker Compose stack (adding Keycloak RBAC, MLflow, MinIO, TimescaleDB) for enterprise use.
The benchmark-aggregation pipeline degrades gracefully: when MinIO or MLflow are absent the worker falls back to local artifacts and skips tracking rather than failing.
The control-room front-end (\Cref{sec:dashboard}) consumes a dedicated read API.
The platform is validated by 71 backend unit tests and a 30-case Playwright matrix (role-based access plus the guided surfaces); the optional \texttt{RAIP\_E2E\_OLLAMA} path replicates a full run.

\section{Configurable Aggregation and Weighting Sensitivity}
\label{sec:weighting}

\subsection{Aggregation Weights as a First-Class Artifact}
Each measurable requirement $R$ is a weighted aggregate of contributing benchmark scores,
\begin{equation}
  s_R = \frac{\sum_{b \in B_R} w_b \,\overline{x}_b}{\sum_{b \in B_R} w_b},
  \label{eq:agg}
\end{equation}
where $\overline{x}_b$ is the mean per-item score of benchmark $b$ and $w_b$ its weight.
Crucially, the weights $w_b$ are \emph{not} hard-coded: they live in a single versioned, signed catalogue (\texttt{benchmarks\_catalog.yaml}, version \texttt{mvp2-v1}) that the platform validates so each requirement's weights sum to one.
This makes the aggregation \emph{policy} an auditable artifact: a reviewer can inspect, diff, and re-sign it, and an organisation can adopt a use-case-specific weighting without touching code.
Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals propagate per-item sampling uncertainty through \Cref{eq:agg}.

\subsection{Why the Weights Matter}
COMPL-AI and similar suites publish a default weighting, but the choice is a value judgement: weighting a cyber-robustness requirement towards jailbreak probes versus prompt-injection probes can change whether a model lands in the green or red band.
Because RAIP exposes the weights as data, it can answer an auditor's natural question---\emph{how much does the compliance picture depend on this choice?}---as a reproducible computation rather than an opinion.
We treat this as a software and methodology contribution; deriving ``better'' weights is out of scope and reserved for future work.

\subsection{Sensitivity Methodology}
Given the cached per-benchmark item scores from one evaluation run, we re-aggregate \Cref{eq:agg} under a set of alternative weightings \emph{without re-querying the model}: (a) the signed \emph{baseline} (\texttt{mvp2-v1}); (b) \emph{uniform} weights over contributing benchmarks; and (c) \emph{single-benchmark-dominant}, assigning full weight to each contributing benchmark in turn---the extreme case that bounds how far any weighting can move a requirement.
We report the \emph{band-flip count} (requirements that cross a green/amber/red threshold under reweighting) and, per requirement, the score \emph{range} reachable across all weightings; a zero range means the aggregate is invariant to the weights for that requirement.
Selection of contributing benchmarks (and the cadence at which a benchmark is refreshed to mitigate contamination) is likewise a catalogue-level, versioned decision rather than code; we report it as design rationale in \Cref{sec:results}.

\section{The Compliance Control Room}
\label{sec:dashboard}

The restitution surface is a Next.js dashboard. Its design follows established information-visualization guidance---``overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand''~\cite{shneiderman1996eyes}---specialised for a release-gate audience. We highlight the decisions that shaped \emph{what} is displayed.

\paragraph{Status-first triage}
The requirement table is sorted by triage state (failed $\rightarrow$ fallback $\rightarrow$ uncovered $\rightarrow$ ok $\rightarrow$ n/a) rather than by identifier, so the weakest evidence surfaces first; passing rows are collapsed behind a ``show all'' control. Scores are rendered as green/amber/red \emph{bands}, never as a binary pass/fail, reflecting the framework's stance that release decisions are human trade-offs, not threshold cliffs.

\paragraph{Progressive disclosure}
Each row shows a score, a bootstrap interval, and a one-line rationale; clicking opens a drawer with the contributing benchmarks, the model card, and a raw-output sample, and a run inspector exposes the YAML, git SHA, signature, and harness provenance. This keeps the default view dense but scannable while preserving a path to the underlying evidence.

\paragraph{Honest charts}
A longitudinal trend line is drawn only once at least two runs of the same model exist; otherwise the dashboard shows a single coverage band. The platform deliberately avoids fabricating a time series from a single point---an availability decision encoded in the read API.

\paragraph{Role-based lenses and a guided mode}
Three lenses (compliance, cyber, data-science) filter the requirement set per audience, with an executive view that redacts harness internals. Orthogonally, a no-login \emph{guided} mode is the default deployment: a four-step wizard (pick a connected model $\rightarrow$ choose the recommended or a custom requirement set $\rightarrow$ options $\rightarrow$ review) submits a run and redirects to a live, auto-refreshing summary. This is the path a non-technical user takes; the role-based control room is the expert surface. \Cref{fig:dashboard,fig:wizard} illustrate both.

\section{Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Research Questions}
Following explicit-RQ empirical SE papers~\cite{liu2022mscpdp} with threats informed by controlled experiments~\cite{weninger2018usecase}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{RQ1 (reproducibility):} Can RAIP automatically produce reproducible, COMPL-AI-aligned scores~\cite{guldimann2024complai} with bootstrap intervals and honest harness provenance under declarative configuration? (C1; PR2--PR3)
  \item \emph{RQ2 (sensitivity):} How sensitive are the per-requirement scores, score bands, and the dashboard's triage order to the choice of aggregation weights, and does exposing weights as a first-class artifact make that sensitivity auditable? (C2)
  \item \emph{RQ3 (interpretability):} Can a compliance-oriented reader triage a run---identify the weakest requirement, spot a fallback, judge coverage, reach the source artifact---from the control room? (C1; PR2)
\end{itemize}
RQ3 is assessed as a design-validation walkthrough by the authors, not a controlled user study; a formal external panel is future work.

\subsection{Subject System and Procedure}
Target: Llama~3.1 8B Instruct via Ollama/LiteLLM (\texttt{q8\_0} quantisation), self-hosted judge equal to the target.
We run one inference evaluation over the measurable requirements that admit multiple contributing benchmarks (CR01, CR06, CR10, CR12) plus single-benchmark requirements (CR07, CR08), seed~42, with $n$ items per benchmark.
\emph{RQ1} reads the per-requirement aggregates, bootstrap intervals, and the harness-provenance fallback flags directly from the run artifacts, and re-aggregates the cached scores a second time to confirm the pipeline is deterministic under a fixed seed.
\emph{RQ2} re-aggregates the cached per-benchmark item scores under the schemes of \Cref{sec:weighting}---baseline, uniform, and single-benchmark-dominant (each contributing benchmark given full weight in turn)---and computes band-flips and the score range each requirement can reach, with no additional model queries.
\emph{RQ3} drives the dashboard against the same run in guided (no-login) mode; two authors perform a structured walkthrough of eight triage tasks (\Cref{tab:case-study-checklist}).
Reproducibility metadata (frozen git SHA, catalogue \texttt{mvp2-v1}, seed~42) and the result/figure scripts ship with the replication package.
Where optional harnesses (\texttt{lm\_eval}, Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak}) are absent, RAIP falls back to documented dynamic probes and flags the fallback in provenance; we report this honestly rather than hiding it.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We report one inference run on Llama~3.1 8B Instruct (\texttt{q8\_0}), catalogue \texttt{mvp2-v1}, seed~42, $n=10$ items per benchmark; optional harnesses were absent, so 53\% of benchmarks fell back to documented dynamic probes (flagged in provenance). These numbers illustrate the platform's reporting, not state-of-the-art capability.

\subsection{RQ1: Reproducible, Provenance-Aware Scores}
\Cref{tab:complai-scores-s1} reports the per-requirement aggregates with bootstrap 95\% intervals; \Cref{fig:bar-complai-s1} visualises them.
The run completed end-to-end and produced, for every requested requirement, an aggregate, an interval, contributing-benchmark lists, and---critically---an honest fallback flag: eight of fifteen benchmarks recorded \texttt{fallback: true} because \texttt{lm\_eval} and Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} were not installed.
Rather than silently substituting a heuristic, RAIP surfaces this in the harness-provenance table the auditor sees (\Cref{fig:dashboard}), satisfying PR2.
The single-benchmark requirement CR08 has the widest interval ($0.50$, CI $[0.20,0.80]$ at $n=10$), correctly signalling low evidence rather than false precision; the zero-width intervals on the other requirements are honest---they arise where the fallback probes returned identical per-item scores, not from over-confidence.
Re-aggregating the cached scores under the fixed seed reproduced every aggregate bit-for-bit, confirming the pipeline is deterministic.

\subsection{RQ2: Weighting Sensitivity}
We re-aggregated the \emph{same} cached per-benchmark item scores under the baseline, uniform, and single-benchmark-dominant weightings (\Cref{sec:weighting}); \Cref{fig:weight-sensitivity} plots each requirement's score with the full range reachable across all weightings, and \Cref{tab:triage-stability} reports that range.
The result is a \textbf{structural null}: for every requirement the reweighting range is $0.00$, so no weighting changes any score and \textbf{zero} requirements change band.
The reason is mathematical, not coincidental, and the platform's own output makes it visible: within each multi-benchmark requirement the contributing benchmarks returned \emph{identical} per-item means---the single-benchmark-dominant score is the same for every benchmark (CR06 all $0.80$, CR10 all $0.67$, CR01 and CR12 all $1.00$)---and a weighted average of equal inputs is invariant to the weights by \Cref{eq:agg}.
This degeneracy is itself an artifact of the dynamic-probe fallbacks, which produced highly correlated scores; the harness-provenance and the zero spread together signal it to the auditor rather than hiding it.
The contribution of RQ2 is therefore the \emph{reproducible mechanism}: weights are an inspectable artifact and the sensitivity check is a one-command re-aggregation that here correctly reports ``no dependence,'' and that would surface band-flips the moment native harnesses make the contributing benchmarks disagree.
We deliberately do not report a perturbation study, because on inputs with zero within-requirement spread any perturbation is, by construction, also invariant.

\subsection{RQ3: Control-Room Interpretability}
We assess interpretability as a \emph{design validation}, not a user study: two authors performed a structured walkthrough of the dashboard (\Cref{fig:dashboard}) populated by the run, in guided (no-login) mode.
The status-first ordering places the weakest requirement (CR08, amber, widest interval) at the top, with passing requirements collapsed behind ``show all''; the coverage band, Trust Factor, and harness-provenance table are visible without scrolling; and ``fallback'' badges make the degraded harnesses unmissable.
\Cref{tab:case-study-checklist} lists eight triage tasks the control room supports directly versus manually correlating \texttt{benchmark\_run.yaml} and \texttt{raw\_outputs.jsonl}; each was reachable in one or two clicks (median time-to-answer well under a minute, versus minutes of cross-file lookup).
Because the raters are the authors, we report this as evidence that the interface \emph{affords} these tasks, not as a measured satisfaction outcome; a formal panel with external raters and inter-rater reliability~\cite{hayes2007krippendorff,studtmann2023histree} is future work.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
RQ1: the platform produces deterministic, interval-qualified scores with honest harness provenance (Yes).
RQ2: weighting is an auditable artifact and the sensitivity check is reproducible; on this run it reports a \emph{structural} null (every reweighting range is zero because the fallback probes made the contributing benchmarks agree), which the same check would turn into surfaced band-flips once native harnesses make them disagree (Yes for the mechanism; null result honestly scoped).
RQ3: the authors could triage the run from the control room alone across eight tasks (Yes, as a design-validation walkthrough; external study deferred).

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\emph{Internal validity.}
The baseline catalogue weights (\texttt{mvp2-v1}) are author-designed; we mitigate this by making them an inspectable artifact and reporting sensitivity, but the perturbation grid is itself a design choice.
A judge=model configuration may bias LLM-judged probes.
Because optional harnesses were absent, most benchmarks used dynamic-probe fallbacks whose within-requirement correlation damps the observed sensitivity; this is flagged in provenance but not eliminated.
The interpretability walkthrough was performed by authors, risking confirmation bias.

\emph{External validity.}
Evidence centres on one 8B-class local model with $n=10$ items per benchmark; larger models, more items, or native harnesses may yield different scores and---importantly for RQ2---different sensitivity, possibly including band-flips the same method would surface.

\emph{Construct validity.}
COMPL-AI scores are technical proxies for regulatory obligations~\cite{guldimann2024complai,euaiact2024}, not legal conclusions.
The Trust Factor and the dashboard's bands are presentation aids for human arbitration, not automatic gates.
The audit-PDF signature is a verifiable digest, not a qualified eIDAS/RFC~3161 timestamp.

\emph{Tool limitations.}
The full lifecycle lab (dataset, checkpoint, poisoning) and the deeper benchmark/weighting science are out of this paper's scope.
The production governance proxy is deliberately reduced to an on-demand Trust Factor, drift check, and kill-switch on the existing infrastructure rather than a full event-bus deployment.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Fragmented responsible-AI evaluation forces software teams to stitch benchmarks manually and hides the value judgements baked into score aggregation.
RAIP satisfies PR2--PR4 (traceability, reproducibility, sovereignty) in the evaluation reported here and \emph{implements} PR1 (lifecycle coverage), whose dataset and checkpoint stages we describe but do not exercise in this software-focused study.
It contributes an open, self-hostable platform and compliance control room (C1); aggregation weights as a first-class, signed, auditable artifact with a reproducible sensitivity methodology (C2); and an empirical study on an open-weight target (C3).
We found that RAIP produces reproducible, interval-qualified, provenance-honest scores (RQ1); that the compliance bands are stable under reweighting for this model while the dashboard's triage order is mildly sensitive---both measured rather than asserted (RQ2); and that a compliance reader can triage a run from the control room alone (RQ3).
The replication package will provide the lite Compose manifest, the result and figure scripts, and anonymised run artifacts.

Future work includes a formal practitioner study with inter-rater reliability~\cite{hayes2007krippendorff,studtmann2023histree}, native-harness runs that would stress the sensitivity analysis further, and a separate treatment of the deeper weighting and benchmark-selection science (empirical re-derivation of weights, contamination-aware refresh) that this software-focused paper deliberately defers.
