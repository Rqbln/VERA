\begin{abstract}
Organisations deploying large language models under the EU AI Act are strongly encouraged to produce reproducible technical evidence across trustworthy-AI principles.
COMPL-AI, a framework introduced in 2024, translates these principles into twelve measurable requirements and reports them as a benchmarking study.
We report our experience building and operating VERA (\emph{Verifiable Evaluation for Responsible AI}), an open-source framework for risk analysis and technical audit of LLM deployments that builds on COMPL-AI, developed and trialed in the responsible-AI function of a large European bank.
Four operational capabilities address gaps we hit in practice.
VERA generates run-tied evidence artifacts that can inform the risk assessments and technical documentation the Act calls for.
It delivers results through a role-based dashboard a non-specialist can drive without code.
It treats the scoring policy behind each final score as a first-class, signed object, and a closed-form bound indicates which verdicts are robust to changes in the scoring weights.
Its specification, benchmarks, and weights are swappable data, so VERA runs COMPL-AI today and can adapt to a different specification without code changes.
In operation, we benchmarked six open-weight LLMs on public datasets and a released synthetic corpus: three model families at a comparable size (Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen2.5~7B, Gemma~2~9B), then three sizes of one family (Ministral~3B, Mistral~7B, Mistral-Small~24B).
Across sizes, capability, a coarse measure of raw performance, increases from 0.70 at 3B and 7B to 1.00 at 24B.
Yet cyber resilience drops from 0.50 to 0.00 at the largest model, and this verdict is invariant to any reweighting: a bigger model is not always a more responsible one.
We share the lessons learned from building and deploying the framework.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Motivation}
Before an organisation ships an LLM-based assistant in a regulated setting, the EU AI Act asks for technical evidence across its trustworthy-AI principles~\cite{euaiact2024}, among them robustness, privacy, transparency, fairness, and human oversight.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai}, a community operationalization of the Act, turns these principles into twelve measurable requirements.
It reports them as an open-source benchmarking suite, decomposed per principle and per benchmark.
This is the right structure, and we build on it.
What it leaves to the practitioner is the last step to a release decision.
The suite is driven from a command line aimed at ML engineers, not packaged for the compliance or risk owner who has to sign off.
Its aggregation fixes one implicit weighting, a simple average, and does not quantify how that choice shapes a verdict.
It does not say how close a passing requirement sits to failing, nor which verdicts a different, equally defensible weighting would flip.
A release decision gains little from one more number.
It calls for a per-criterion view whose scoring policy is explicit and auditable, and a statement of which verdicts are robust to it.
VERA grew out of this exact need in the responsible-AI function of a large European bank, where release decisions on LLM-based assistants sit with compliance and risk owners under internal-control obligations; it was built in an industry-academia collaboration and is released as open source.

\subsection{Contributions}
We present VERA, an open-source framework that runs the twelve measurable COMPL-AI requirements, makes the aggregation policy behind each score signed and auditable, and quantifies each verdict's robustness to that policy.
The six non-measurable requirements follow a traceable human path in the same tool: a rubric-based review queue, a measured energy report, and signed declarative forms.
Scores stay explainable in the decompositional sense: each requirement score is traceable, inside the tool, to the contributing public benchmarks and their individual results.
VERA provides four operational capabilities, each addressing a gap we hit in practice, each tied to a research question, and each still a concrete delta over the two closest works, the COMPL-AI reference implementation and PromptOps~\cite{sunetnanta2025promptops}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1 (Operable dashboard).} VERA delivers COMPL-AI's measurable metrics\footnote{COMPL-AI project: \url{https://compl-ai.org}.} through a role-based dashboard that a non-specialist can drive end-to-end, launch a run and read its verdicts, without writing code (RQ1). We support this claim with a small self-administered user study ($n{=}5$) in \Cref{sec:results}. COMPL-AI ships a command-line study and a public per-principle leaderboard aimed at ML engineers; PromptOps reports flat score tables. The delta is operability for a non-specialist, not visualisation as such.
  \item \textbf{C2 (Run-tied traceability).} We bind run-tied evidence artifacts to the evaluation that produced them: each run generates its own model card and, for dataset runs, a datasheet, populated from that exact evaluation; COMPL-AI emits no such run-tied artifacts. These artifacts can inform a provider's risk assessment and its Annex~IV technical documentation; they do not constitute or replace either. Within the dashboard, every verdict also decomposes into its contributing benchmarks, so a non-specialist sees \emph{why} a requirement is weak (RQ2). The decomposition itself follows COMPL-AI's hierarchical reporting; the research delta is the binding of decomposition, run, and signed documentation into one auditable object.
The same run-tied path covers the six non-measurable requirements: each run auto-queues a rubric-based human review for explainability (N01) and corrigibility (N02), measures energy for N03, and attests N04 to N06 through signed declarative forms.
  \item \textbf{C3 (Auditable, swappable scoring).} The scoring policy is a versioned, signed catalog whose SHA-256 digest is pinned into every run, and a closed-form bound (\Cref{prop:range}) quantifies how much each verdict depends on it (RQ4), where COMPL-AI fixes an unweighted average and leaves that dependence unmeasured. The requirement specification, the benchmarks, and the weights are themselves swappable data, and we exercise this: an alternative security-focused specification ships as two data files and ran end to end through configuration alone (RQ3). This is why we call it a framework.
  \item \textbf{C4 (Reproducible study).} We report an open, reproducible study of the twelve requirements over two model panels, three families at a comparable size and one family across three sizes, on public datasets and a released synthetic corpus. The value is the signed, reproducible protocol across families and sizes, applied to the models we actually evaluate in practice; the cross-model results are findings.
\end{itemize}
We evaluate C1--C4 across the two panels of \Cref{sec:results}, where VERA flags concrete responsible-AI gaps between families and sizes.

VERA is open-source and available at \url{\repourl}. The released replication package (seed~42) regenerates every table and figure in this paper, including the signed catalog and the verdict-sensitivity study.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Regulatory Framing and COMPL-AI}
The EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact2024} follows a risk-based approach.
It bans a narrow set of practices; it imposes risk management, technical documentation, and human oversight on the high-risk systems listed in its Annex~III; it sets transparency duties for limited-risk uses such as AI assistants (Art.~50); and it leaves minimal-risk systems largely free.
Technical evidence is asked of high-risk systems, and the ethical principles behind these obligations draw on the non-binding HLEG guidelines~\cite{hleg2019trustworthyai}.
NIST and ISO frameworks~\cite{nist2023airmf,iso42001_2023} guide organizational risk management, but stop short of runnable metrics.
Prior software-engineering work extracts and checks regulatory requirements with NLP and LLM techniques, including on the AI Act itself~\cite{abualhaija2025llmregextraction,amaral2022nlpcompliance,abualhaija2024regchange}.
VERA is complementary: it does not parse the regulation but operationalizes an existing requirement taxonomy into a runnable, auditable evaluation.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} narrows the metrics gap.
It is an independent effort by ETH Zurich, INSAIT, and LatticeFlow AI: it is not EU-funded, it is not part of the Act's harmonised standards, and a European Commission spokesperson described it as a first step toward operationalizing the Act.
It groups the Act's ethical principles into six areas and maps them to eighteen technical requirements, twelve with measurable scores in $[0,1]$.
It also ships its own open-source evaluation suite (27 benchmarks) and a public leaderboard.
The six remaining requirements stay unassessed in its study: the authors note that adequate measurement tools do not yet exist, and report them as N/A or as trivial constants.
We state the overlap plainly: VERA implements COMPL-AI's measurable taxonomy and runs the same family of public benchmarks.
COMPL-AI already reports results decompositionally, as a hierarchy of principle, requirement, and benchmark with sample-level logs and a public per-principle view, and it is community-extensible; VERA claims neither decomposition nor extensibility as novel.
VERA's deltas are four properties COMPL-AI does not provide.
First, a signed, digest-pinned scoring policy with a closed-form verdict-sensitivity analysis: COMPL-AI aggregates each requirement by an unweighted average and does not quantify how that choice shapes a verdict.
Second, run-tied model cards and datasheets.
Third, an operable, role-based interface for non-specialists, with an optional runtime-governance mode.
Fourth, a traceable human path for the six non-measurable requirements, a rubric-based review queue plus signed attestation forms, where COMPL-AI stops at N/A.

\subsection{Benchmark Suites and Metrics}
VERA reuses established public suites rather than proposing new benchmark science.
Capability (CR06) draws on MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}, GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, HumanEval~\cite{chen2021humaneval}, TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa}, and BBH~\cite{suzgun2023bbh}.
Calibration (CR07) uses expected calibration error~\cite{guo2017calibration}.
Fairness and bias (CR10--CR11) use BBQ~\cite{parrish2022bbq}, BOLD~\cite{dhamala2021bold}, StereoSet~\cite{nadeem2021stereoset}, and disparity metrics~\cite{hardt2016equality,wang2023decodingtrust}.
Toxicity (CR12) uses RealToxicityPrompts~\cite{gehman2020realtoxicity}.
Dataset-stage checks (CR03--CR05) apply corpus heuristics; membership and extraction attacks~\cite{carlini2021membership,carlini2023extracting} motivate simplified checks.
Watermarking (CR09) follows a statistical heuristic inspired by~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}, not production SynthID.
Each suite answers a measurement question in isolation.
None packages them for a non-specialist or explains a verdict, which is what VERA adds.

\subsection{Documentation Supporting Risk Assessment}
The EU AI Act expects technical documentation for high-risk systems, with a content list fixed by law (Art.~11, Annex~IV).
The community reuses two formats: Model Cards~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard} and Datasheets for Datasets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets}.
These documents are often produced after the run, which can let them drift from the evaluation they describe.
VERA instead generates a model card, and a datasheet for dataset runs, directly from each evaluation.
The artifact therefore stays tied to the exact run that produced it; it is supporting evidence that can inform the Annex~IV documentation, not a replacement for it.

\subsection{Open-Source LLM Evaluation Frameworks}
Several open-source frameworks evaluate LLMs, each at a different layer.
HELM~\cite{liang2023helm} standardizes which scenarios to report.
The lm-evaluation suite~\cite{gao2021lmevalharness} standardizes how a benchmark is executed.
Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} probes jailbreaks and prompt injection, and DecodingTrust~\cite{wang2023decodingtrust} is a trustworthiness suite.
These are strong measurement building blocks, and VERA reuses them rather than competing with them.
What they leave out is a requirement-level, explainable view for a non-specialist and a modular way to swap the underlying specification.
VERA supplies that layer on top of the COMPL-AI taxonomy.

\subsection{Software Engineering Tool Papers}
\Cref{tab:related-work} positions VERA against neighboring tools.
PromptOps~\cite{sunetnanta2025promptops} is one of the recent LLM-trustworthiness tools.
It scores a model prompt by prompt and reports a flat set of numbers, without data-stage coverage, per-criterion explanation, or a modular taxonomy.
VERA differs on three axes: it maps results to the full COMPL-AI CR01--CR12 taxonomy, it explains each verdict by decomposing it across criteria and public benchmarks, and its registry and taxonomy are pluggable so the framework adapts beyond COMPL-AI.
Explicit research-question structures~\cite{liu2022mscpdp} and controlled-experiment design~\cite{weninger2018usecase} inform our evaluation layout.

\section{VERA Framework and Architecture}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Conceptual Overview}
VERA turns a model and a chosen set of responsible-AI requirements into an explainable scorecard that different roles can read.
\Cref{fig:journey} shows the journey.
A user picks a connected model, chooses which requirements to check, and launches a run.
VERA then evaluates each requirement with public benchmarks, aggregates the results into a per-criterion score, and presents them in a role-based dashboard that explains every verdict.

The founding idea is that a single global compliance number is semantically thin and even hazardous.
It hides why a model fails, and it creates regulatory cliffs where a small change flips a pass into a fail.
Responsible AI, moreover, does not mean the same thing to every actor.
VERA therefore replaces the single number with a per-criterion view and role-segregated dashboards (\Cref{sec:dashboard}) for three profiles: a legal or compliance officer, a responsible-AI officer, and a data or ML engineer.
Concretely, a target can be a model endpoint, a checkpoint, or a dataset, tagged with a lifecycle stage in a declarative run file.
The next section details the engine that runs it (\Cref{fig:architecture}).

\subsection{System Architecture}
\Cref{fig:architecture} shows how VERA turns a launch request into a scored, documented run.
A user enters through one of three surfaces: the \texttt{vera-eval} command line, the \texttt{POST /runs} REST endpoint, or the no-login guided wizard.
All three enqueue the same declarative job on a background task queue (Celery and Redis), which also holds the run store and the kill-switch.
A worker then runs a two-step controller (LangGraph): an \texttt{evaluate} step and an \texttt{aggregate} step.
The \texttt{evaluate} step resolves each requirement to its benchmarks through a registry, dispatches each to its runner, and routes inference through a self-hosted model router (LiteLLM) to the target and a judge.
The \texttt{aggregate} step combines the cached per-item scores into one score per requirement, with bootstrap intervals, using the modular benchmark catalog.
The run emits its artifacts, the run file, a model card, per-item outputs, and a content signature, to object storage or the local filesystem, and a read API serves them to the dashboard (\Cref{sec:dashboard}).

\subsection{Responsible-AI Pillars}
Having shown how the pipeline runs, we state what it covers.
The pillars are not ours to choose: they are the six principle areas COMPL-AI derives from the Act's ethical principles~\cite{guldimann2024complai,euaiact2024}, and \Cref{tab:pillars} maps each to the requirements VERA covers and the components that evaluate them.
The distinction between model and system runs through the table: the measurable requirements check the model in isolation, which is also COMPL-AI's stated scope, while the N requirements and the run-tied artifacts address the surrounding system, whose conformity the Act ultimately regulates.
Transparency does cover explainability: its measurable slice spans capabilities (CR06), calibration and interpretability (CR07), AI disclosure (CR08), and traceability (CR09), but judging an actual explanation still resists automation.
VERA therefore routes explainability (N01) and corrigibility (N02) to a documented human review, and groups them under human agency and oversight, the pillar COMPL-AI leaves without technical requirements because it is system-level.
That pillar is where the human stays in the loop: the review queue, the role-based views, and the kill-switch (\Cref{sec:dashboard}) are its operational surface.

\subsection{Benchmark Provenance}
The catalog (\texttt{benchmarks\_catalog.yaml}) defines, per requirement, which public benchmarks contribute and with which weights; it is versioned, so a weighting change is a new, diffable revision rather than a code edit.
It is a versioned, signed artifact: every run pins the catalog's SHA-256 digest next to its scores, so a score records the exact weighting that produced it.
A benchmark without a catalog weight is excluded from the aggregate rather than silently defaulted, and the registry-to-catalog mapping is validated at run start, so every aggregate reproduces exactly from its decomposition.
Each per-item output records which benchmark and which runner produced it.
An explicit flag marks the case where an optional engine was absent and a simpler check ran instead, so a reader can see where each number came from.

\subsection{Data-Stage Engines}
Dataset scans populate CR03--CR05 in-graph: toxicity via Detoxify~\cite{hanu2020detoxify}, copyright via memorisation heuristics, and privacy via Presidio~\cite{microsoft2023presidio} PII detection.
They run on the synthetic corpus of \Cref{sec:generated}.
Checkpoint evaluation and a data-poisoning lab also exist in the platform but are out of scope for this paper (Appendix~\ref{app:lifecycle}).

\subsection{Governance Artifact Generation}
Each run leaves a documentation trail.
Model cards follow the Model Cards format~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard}: twelve measurable rows, the non-measurable statuses, dataset evaluation URIs, benchmark provenance, signatures, and limitations.
Datasheets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets} attach to corpus scans.
Environmental hooks record per-run energy and emissions for N03.
A signed audit export gathers the full N01--N06 state: the live review results for N01 and N02, the measured energy, and the attested forms.

\subsection{Implementation and Deployment Profiles}
The \texttt{vera} package targets Python~3.11 and ships three deployment profiles over one code base: a single-command \emph{lite} stack (Redis, API, worker, and a Next.js dashboard, local artifacts, no login); a full \emph{enterprise} Docker Compose stack (adding Keycloak RBAC, MLflow, MinIO, TimescaleDB); and an optional runtime-governance overlay that supervises a deployed model live (inline proxy, policy layer, kill-switch).
In internal trials the overlay ran in shadow and advisory modes; advisory, which flags low-trust responses without blocking, matched the institution's internal-control cadence, and the one-click kill-switch gives the compliance owner an immediate stop.
The runtime itself stays out of scope for this paper.
Every layer degrades gracefully: absent MinIO or MLflow fall back to local artifacts and no tracking, so partial deployments still run.
The dashboard front-end (\Cref{sec:dashboard}) consumes a dedicated read API and an admin control plane.
The platform is validated by 143 backend unit tests and a 38-case Playwright matrix (role-based access, the guided surfaces, the HITL review panel, the governance panel, the language toggle, and the study module); the optional \texttt{VERA\_E2E\_OLLAMA} path replicates a full run.

\section{The VERA Control Room}
\label{sec:dashboard}
The dashboard is contribution C1: it turns the requirement scores into a view a non-specialist can read, following ``overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand''~\cite{shneiderman1996eyes}.

\paragraph{Score at a glance}
The summary opens on a deliberately uncluttered hero view rather than a wall of tables (\Cref{fig:dashboard}).
A semicircular gauge places the Trust Factor on three segments aligned with the band thresholds (red below 0.40, amber to 0.70, green above), next to the verdict counts and the weakest requirements.
The remaining detail, run metadata, benchmark provenance, and trends, stays one click away behind collapsed sections, so the first screen carries only the information a release decision relies on.
The complete screens, including the expanded detail view, are in Appendix~\ref{app:dashboard}.

\paragraph{Status-first triage}
The requirement table is sorted by state (failed, fallback, uncovered, ok, n/a) rather than by identifier, so the weakest evidence surfaces first, and passing rows collapse behind a ``show all'' control.
Scores are shown as green, amber, and red bands, not a binary pass or fail, because release decisions are human trade-offs rather than threshold cliffs.

\paragraph{Explaining a score}
This is contribution C2.
Each row shows a score, an interval, and a one-line rationale.
Clicking a row opens a drawer with the contributing public benchmarks, the model card, and a sample output, so a reader sees \emph{why} a requirement is weak, not only that it is.
A run inspector exposes the run file, the git SHA, the signature, and the per-benchmark provenance.

\paragraph{Charts}
A trend line is drawn only once at least two runs of the same model exist; otherwise the dashboard shows a single coverage band.
It does not fabricate a time series from a single point.

\paragraph{Role-based views and a guided mode}
Three views (responsible-AI, cyber, and data-science) filter the requirement set per role, with an executive view that hides internal detail.
A no-login guided mode is the default: a four-step wizard (pick a connected model, choose a recommended or custom requirement set, set options, review) submits a run and opens a live summary.
This is the path a non-technical user takes, while the role-based views are the expert surface (\Cref{fig:dashboard}; more screens in Appendix~\ref{app:dashboard}).

\section{Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Research Questions}
We organise the evaluation around four practical questions, what we wanted the tool to do and whether it does, stated explicitly as in prior empirical software-engineering work~\cite{liu2022mscpdp,weninger2018usecase}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{RQ1 (usable dashboard).} Can a non-specialist drive a run and read its results from the dashboard alone, without writing code? (C1)
  \item \emph{RQ2 (explainable scores).} Does VERA show \emph{why} a requirement is weak, by decomposing the score across criteria and the public benchmarks behind it? (C2)
  \item \emph{RQ3 (modular coverage).} Does one framework evaluate the twelve requirements across different model families and sizes, and adapt to a different specification without code changes? (C3, C4)
  \item \emph{RQ4 (verdict robustness).} How much does each requirement's verdict depend on the aggregation weighting, and which verdicts are invariant to it? (C3)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RQ4: How Much Does the Verdict Depend on the Weighting?}
\label{sec:rq4}
Each requirement score is a weighted mean of its per-benchmark means, $s_R(w)=\sum_{b\in B_R} w_b\, m_b$ with $w_b\ge 0$ and $\sum_b w_b=1$, read from the signed catalog.
The score is therefore a convex combination of the $m_b$, which yields a closed-form robustness statement.

\begin{proposition}[Reachable range under reweighting]
\label{prop:range}
For any admissible weighting $w$, $\min_{b} m_b \le s_R(w) \le \max_{b} m_b$, and both bounds are attained by the one-hot weightings.
Over the entire simplex of weightings, the reachable range of $s_R$ is therefore exactly $\Delta_R=\max_b m_b-\min_b m_b$.
\end{proposition}

Two consequences carry the audit value.
If $\Delta_R=0$, or the interval $[\min_b m_b,\max_b m_b]$ lies inside one band, the verdict is \emph{invariant}: no reweighting can flip it.
If the interval straddles a band threshold ($0.40$ or $0.70$), the verdict is \emph{weight-dependent}: an admissible reweighting flips it.
\Cref{tab:sensitivity} and \Cref{fig:sensitivity} report $\Delta_R$ and the flip flag for every multi-benchmark requirement on both panels, computed by one command from the cached decompositions, with no model re-queries.

The result is non-degenerate, and it cuts both ways.
We observe that robustness (CR01) is invariant on every model: its verdict does not depend on the weighting.
Capability (CR06), representation bias (CR10), and toxicity (CR12) are weight-dependent on most models ($\Delta{=}1.00$): the same cached results aggregate into a green or a red verdict purely by choice of weights.
On the size panel the largest model inverts the pattern: its capability verdict becomes invariant while its bias verdict stays weight-dependent, which supports the side observation that a bigger model is not always a more responsible one.
A reviewer therefore reads \Cref{tab:panelA,tab:panelB} together with \Cref{tab:sensitivity}: the former gives the verdicts, the latter says which of them would survive a different weighting.
An empirical sweep corroborates the worst-case bound: across 200 random perturbations of the catalog weights, moderate noise ($\sigma{=}0.1$) already flips $0.5$ bands per draw on average (up to two), and stronger noise ($\sigma{=}0.2$) reorders the dashboard's triage ranking in $64\%$ of draws.

The bound also changes decisions between defensible policies, not only under noise.
We re-aggregated the cached panel results under three named policies: the uniform average COMPL-AI fixes, our signed catalog, and the shipped security-first alternative that upweights adversarial benchmarks.
Four of thirty verdicts flip (\Cref{tab:policies}), all on toxicity (CR12): the same cached results read green under the uniform policy and amber under the security-first one.
A single unweighted number reports the green verdict without disclosing this dependence; the signed catalog together with \Cref{prop:range} makes it visible and auditable.

\subsection{Limitations}
The user study is small ($n{=}5$) and its participants were AI-literate researchers from the host institution rather than compliance officers, so the non-specialist claim rests on the design plus this first trial; a judge model (Llama~3.1~8B) may also bias the LLM-judged probes.
CR06 and CR10 use dynamic probes under the Ollama log-probability constraint rather than native log-likelihood scoring; this is recorded in provenance, and a vLLM backend would let the native path corroborate those scores.
The panels cover open-weight models from 3B to 24B at $n{=}10$ items per benchmark and one seed, so larger models, more items, or more seeds may shift the numbers.
We frame the study as an illustration of the method, not a broad empirical benchmark; the signed, one-command protocol makes such extensions mechanical.
Like COMPL-AI, VERA does not score the six non-measurable requirements automatically; its contribution there is a traceable human-review and attestation path, not a new metric, and the panel tables report only the twelve measurable requirements.
The catalog weights are author-designed; \Cref{prop:range} bounds the effect of reweighting them, not of selecting different benchmarks.
The COMPL-AI scores are technical proxies for regulatory obligations~\cite{guldimann2024complai,euaiact2024}, not legal conclusions, and the bands are aids for human judgement, not automatic gates.
Whether a given deployment falls under the Act's obligations, and how, stays a jurisdiction-specific legal question; this paper provides technical groundwork, not legal interpretation.
The governance-runtime benchmark measures per-request overhead and detection on planted cases, not sustained production load.

\section{Generated Data and a Banking Application}
\label{sec:application}
This section covers two complements to the model benchmark: the synthetic corpus we generate for the dataset-stage requirements, and one regulated deployment setting that illustrates how the same run is read by a sector reviewer.

\subsection{Generated Data}
\label{sec:generated}
The dataset-stage requirements CR03--CR05 run on a corpus that is fully synthetic: we generated every document, and no real customer or institutional data was used.
A seeded generator produces 230 short banking documents, released as \texttt{banking\_synth.jsonl} (FR/EN retail, corporate, wealth, and complaint records), that plant the failure modes the data stage should catch: synthetic IBANs (\texttt{FR76} plus 23 digits), RIBs, e-mails, and phones for privacy; near-duplicate pairs for copyright; and imbalanced client segments for data adequacy.
The native engines then score privacy CR05 $=0.73$ (Presidio recall on the planted PII), copyright CR04 $=0.94$ (Levenshtein and BLEU near-duplicate detection), and data adequacy CR03 $=0.84$ (group-balance heuristics).
Because the scans run on the corpus, not the model, these scores are model-independent, and the generator (\texttt{gen\_banking\_corpus.py}) makes them fully reproducible.

\subsection{Application in Banking}
VERA is generic: the models it evaluates are general-purpose, not banking-specific, and so is the framework.
Banking is therefore an illustration of use in a regulated sector, not the scope of the framework.
Consider a release gate for a retail-banking assistant (account FAQs, product explanations, complaint triage).
Under the Act's risk-based approach such an assistant is not a high-risk system: it carries the transparency duties of Art.~50, while the banking use the Act lists as high-risk (Annex~III) is credit scoring.
The binding requirements on the assistant come instead from the bank's internal-control and model-risk obligations, which a second line of defence already tracks: DORA~\cite{dora2022}, the EBA ICT guidelines~\cite{eba2021ictrisk}, and the ACPR's work on AI in finance~\cite{acpr2020ai}; Appendix~\ref{app:banking} gives the detailed mapping.
The same run therefore yields technical evidence these internal controls can consume, and artifacts a banking reviewer recognises.

\section{Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:lessons}
Building and operating VERA inside a regulated institution taught us five lessons that transfer beyond this framework.

\textbf{A single global score is a trap in practice.}
The aggregation policy turned out to be a governance choice, not a technical detail: four of thirty verdicts flip between two defensible weightings (\Cref{tab:policies}), on the same cached results.
The policy has to be visible, signed, and challengeable, or the verdict quietly encodes someone's unexamined preferences.

\textbf{The specification must be data, not code.}
An early version hard-coded the catalog path, and our modularity claim ran ahead of the code.
Externalizing the registry and the signed catalog into configuration made the claim testable, and made every scoring policy auditable by diff.

\textbf{Non-specialists do not adopt command lines.}
The guided, no-login dashboard came from watching compliance and risk owners disengage from CLI workflows; operability, not visualisation, drove adoption.

\textbf{Graceful degradation is a deployment requirement, not a nicety.}
Real environments miss token log-probabilities, optional engines, or GPU kernels; explicit fallback flags kept runs honest where silent degradation would have corrupted the evidence.

\textbf{Run-tied artifacts remove a real source of audit friction.}
Documentation written after the fact drifts from the evaluation it describes; generating the model card and the datasheet from the run itself made that drift structurally impossible.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The EU AI Act asks organisations for technical evidence across trustworthy-AI principles.
COMPL-AI translated the Act into eighteen technical requirements and benchmarked the twelve measurable ones on public datasets.
VERA takes the next step and turns that reference into an operational framework.
It runs the same twelve requirements on public datasets and a released synthetic corpus.
It makes the scoring policy signed and auditable, and reports which verdicts are robust to reweighting.
It emits run-tied evidence artifacts and routes the six non-measurable requirements to a traceable human path.
No neighbouring tool provides these properties together: HELM and lm-eval standardise measurement, COMPL-AI fixes an implicit weighting, and none of them packages the result for a non-specialist.
We provide first evidence, from a small self-administered user study, that a reader can drive a run from the dashboard alone (RQ1), and each verdict decomposes into the public evidence behind it (RQ2).
We further report that one framework covers the twelve requirements across model families and sizes with no code change (RQ3), and that each verdict's dependence on the weighting is bounded in closed form (RQ4).
Across both panels VERA flags concrete responsible-AI gaps, and it exposes a watermarking requirement that no open model meets.
We release VERA as open-source software, available at \url{\repourl}, to speed up adoption and disseminate responsible-AI best practice.
Future work includes a formal practitioner study with inter-rater reliability~\cite{hayes2007krippendorff,studtmann2023histree}, native log-probability runs on a vLLM backend, and the role-based, per-persona scoring that this software-focused paper only sketches.
