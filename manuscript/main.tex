\begin{abstract}
Deploying large language models under the EU AI Act requires reproducible technical evidence across trustworthy-AI principles.
Yet the public metrics that operationalise these principles are scattered across research code, and a single global score hides why a model passes or fails.
We present VERA, an open-source framework that implements the measurable COMPL-AI requirements in a visual, role-based dashboard.
It explains each verdict by decomposing it across responsible-AI criteria and the public benchmarks behind it, and its requirement specification and scoring policy are swappable, so a different responsible-AI specification plugs in.
We benchmark it across model families and sizes on public datasets; the scores separate the models on capability, fairness, and toxicity, and expose a watermarking requirement that no open model meets.
Unlike prior tools that score a model one prompt at a time and leave the reasoning opaque, VERA also covers the data stage and makes every score explainable and reproducible.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}
Before an organisation ships an LLM-based assistant in a regulated setting, the EU AI Act asks for technical evidence across trustworthy-AI principles~\cite{euaiact2024,hleg2019trustworthyai}: robustness, privacy, transparency, and fairness.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai}, a community operationalization of the Act welcomed by the European Commission, turns these principles into twelve measurable requirements.
It is published as a benchmarking study, however, not as a tool a practitioner can operate.
The metrics themselves live in research repositories, each on its own scale, and none is packaged for a non-specialist to run.
Worse, once they are folded into a single global score, that score is opaque: it does not say \emph{why} a model is weak, and a small change can tip it across a threshold.
A release decision does not need another number.
It needs a per-criterion view: where a model fails, and which public benchmark says so.

\subsection{Contributions}
We present VERA (\emph{Verifiable Evaluation for Responsible AI}), an open-source framework that implements the twelve measurable COMPL-AI requirements and makes each score explainable.
By \emph{explainable} we mean decompositional: each requirement score is traceable, inside the tool, to the contributing public benchmarks and their individual results.
We release VERA as open-source software to speed up adoption and disseminate responsible-AI best practice.\footnote{Repository, demo video, and replication package withheld for double-blind review.}
We make four contributions, each tied to a research question and each a concrete delta over the two closest works, the COMPL-AI reference implementation and PromptOps~\cite{sunetnanta2025promptops}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1 (Dashboard).} To our knowledge VERA is the first to implement COMPL-AI's measurable metrics\footnote{COMPL-AI project: \url{https://compl-ai.org}.} behind a visual, role-based dashboard that a non-specialist can drive without writing code (RQ1). COMPL-AI ships a command-line study and a public leaderboard; PromptOps reports flat score tables.
  \item \textbf{C2 (Explainability and traceability).} VERA does not stop at a global score: every verdict decomposes into the responsible-AI criteria and the public benchmarks behind it, so a user sees \emph{why} a model is, for instance, not fair (RQ2). Each run also generates its own model card and, for dataset runs, a datasheet, tied to that exact run, toward the Act's technical-documentation duty (Art.~11). Neither closest work explains verdicts or emits run-tied documentation.
  \item \textbf{C3 (Modularity).} The requirement specification and the scoring policy are first-class, swappable artifacts, data rather than code: VERA runs COMPL-AI today and adapts to a different responsible-AI specification without code changes (RQ3). This is why we call it a framework.
  \item \textbf{C4 (Benchmark).} We contribute an open, reproducible benchmark of the twelve requirements over two model panels, three families at a comparable size and one family across three sizes, on public datasets and a released synthetic corpus.
\end{itemize}
We evaluate C1--C4 across the two panels of \Cref{sec:results}, where VERA flags concrete responsible-AI gaps between families and sizes.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Regulatory Framing and COMPL-AI}
The EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact2024} and the six HLEG trustworthy-AI principles~\cite{hleg2019trustworthyai} ask for technical evidence on high-risk systems.
NIST and ISO frameworks~\cite{nist2023airmf,iso42001_2023} guide organizational risk management, but stop short of runnable metrics.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} narrows that gap.
Developed by ETH Zurich, INSAIT, and LatticeFlow AI, and welcomed by the European Commission as a first step toward operationalizing the Act, it maps the principles to eighteen technical requirements, twelve with measurable scores in $[0,1]$.
It also ships its own open-source evaluation suite (27 benchmarks) and a public leaderboard.
We state the overlap plainly: VERA implements COMPL-AI's measurable taxonomy and runs the same family of public benchmarks.
What COMPL-AI does not offer is a tool a non-specialist can operate.
VERA adds that layer: an orchestration pipeline with a role-based dashboard, decompositional explanation of each verdict, data-stage coverage, run-tied documentation, and a swappable specification.

\subsection{Benchmark Suites and Metrics}
VERA reuses established public suites rather than proposing new benchmark science.
Capability (CR06) draws on MMLU~\cite{hendrycks2021mmlu}, GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, HumanEval~\cite{chen2021humaneval}, TruthfulQA~\cite{lin2022truthfulqa}, and BBH~\cite{suzgun2023bbh}.
Calibration (CR07) uses expected calibration error~\cite{guo2017calibration}.
Fairness and bias (CR10--CR11) use BBQ~\cite{parrish2022bbq}, BOLD~\cite{dhamala2021bold}, StereoSet~\cite{nadeem2021stereoset}, and disparity metrics~\cite{hardt2016equality,wang2023decodingtrust}.
Toxicity (CR12) uses RealToxicityPrompts~\cite{gehman2020realtoxicity}.
Dataset-stage checks (CR03--CR05) apply corpus heuristics; membership and extraction attacks~\cite{carlini2021membership,carlini2023extracting} motivate simplified checks.
Watermarking (CR09) follows a statistical heuristic inspired by~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark}, not production SynthID.
Each suite answers a measurement question in isolation.
None packages them for a non-specialist or explains a verdict, which is what VERA adds.

\subsection{Governance Artifacts}
The EU AI Act expects technical documentation for high-risk systems (Art.~11, Annex~IV).
The community reuses two formats: Model Cards~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard} and Datasheets for Datasets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets}.
These documents are often produced after the run, which can let them drift from the evaluation they describe.
VERA instead generates a model card, and a datasheet for dataset runs, directly from each evaluation.
The artifact therefore stays tied to the exact run that produced it.

\subsection{Open-Source LLM Evaluation Frameworks}
Several open-source frameworks evaluate LLMs, each at a different layer.
HELM~\cite{liang2023helm} standardizes which scenarios to report.
The lm-evaluation suite~\cite{gao2021lmevalharness} standardizes how a benchmark is executed.
Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} probes jailbreaks and prompt injection, and DecodingTrust~\cite{wang2023decodingtrust} is a trustworthiness suite.
These are strong measurement building blocks, and VERA reuses them rather than competing with them.
What they leave out is a requirement-level, explainable view for a non-specialist and a modular way to swap the underlying specification.
VERA supplies that layer on top of the COMPL-AI taxonomy.

\subsection{Software Engineering Tool Papers}
\Cref{tab:related-work} positions VERA against neighboring tools.
PromptOps~\cite{sunetnanta2025promptops} is one of the recent LLM-trustworthiness tools.
It scores a model prompt by prompt and reports a flat set of numbers, without data-stage coverage, per-criterion explanation, or a modular taxonomy.
VERA differs on three axes: it maps results to the full COMPL-AI CR01--CR12 taxonomy, it explains each verdict by decomposing it across criteria and public benchmarks, and its registry and taxonomy are pluggable so the framework adapts beyond COMPL-AI.
Explicit research-question structures~\cite{liu2022mscpdp} and controlled-experiment design~\cite{weninger2018usecase} inform our evaluation layout.

\section{VERA Framework and Architecture}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Conceptual Overview}
VERA turns a model and a chosen set of responsible-AI requirements into an explainable scorecard that different roles can read.
\Cref{fig:journey} shows the journey.
A user picks a connected model, chooses which requirements to check, and launches a run.
VERA then evaluates each requirement with public benchmarks, aggregates the results into a per-criterion score, and presents them in a role-based dashboard that explains every verdict.

The founding idea is that a single global compliance number is semantically thin and even hazardous.
It hides why a model fails, and it creates regulatory cliffs where a small change flips a pass into a fail.
Responsible AI, moreover, does not mean the same thing to every actor.
VERA therefore replaces the single number with a per-criterion view and role-segregated dashboards (\Cref{sec:dashboard}) for three profiles: a legal or compliance officer, a responsible-AI officer, and a data or ML engineer.

Concretely, a target can be a model endpoint, a checkpoint, or a dataset, tagged with a lifecycle stage in a declarative run file.
The next section details the engine that runs it (\Cref{fig:architecture}).

\subsection{System Architecture}
\Cref{fig:architecture} shows how VERA turns a launch request into a scored, documented run.
A user enters through one of three surfaces: the \texttt{vera-eval} command line, the \texttt{POST /runs} REST endpoint, or the no-login guided wizard.
All three enqueue the same declarative job on a background task queue (Celery and Redis), which also holds the run store and the kill-switch.
A worker then runs a two-step controller (LangGraph): an \texttt{evaluate} step and an \texttt{aggregate} step.
The \texttt{evaluate} step resolves each requirement to its benchmarks through a registry, dispatches each to its runner, and routes inference through a self-hosted model router (LiteLLM) to the target and a judge.
The \texttt{aggregate} step combines the cached per-item scores into one score per requirement, with bootstrap intervals, using the modular benchmark catalog.
The run emits its artifacts, the run file, a model card, per-item outputs, and a content signature, to object storage or the local filesystem, and a read API serves them to the dashboard (\Cref{sec:dashboard}).

\subsection{Benchmark Provenance}
The modular catalog (\texttt{benchmarks\_catalog.yaml}) defines, per requirement, which public benchmarks contribute and how they combine.
Each per-item output records which benchmark and which runner produced it.
An explicit flag marks the case where an optional engine was absent and a simpler check ran instead, so a reader always sees where a number came from.

\subsection{Data-Stage Engines}
Dataset scans populate CR03--CR05 in-graph: toxicity via Detoxify~\cite{hanu2020detoxify}, copyright via memorisation heuristics, and privacy via Presidio~\cite{microsoft2023presidio} PII detection.
They run on the synthetic corpus of \Cref{sec:generated}.
Checkpoint evaluation and a data-poisoning lab also exist in the platform but are out of scope for this paper (\Cref{app:lifecycle}).

\subsection{Governance Runtime (Profile-Gated)}
\label{sec:gaas}
Evaluation asks whether a model is responsible now.
Production also needs to know whether it stays responsible.
The enterprise profile therefore adds an optional governance-as-a-service (GaaS) runtime around the same pipeline (\Cref{fig:gaas}), built from open-source parts and reusing the platform's scoring code.
An inline proxy sits in front of the target.
On the synchronous path it consults the policy layer, forwards the request through the model router, and returns the response; off-band it publishes the request and response to an event bus.
Four asynchronous agents consume that stream, cyber (CR02), ethics (CR12, Detoxify~\cite{hanu2020detoxify}), privacy (CR05, Presidio~\cite{microsoft2023presidio}), and drift (CR01), and emit scored signals that a streaming aggregator folds into a live Trust Factor.
An Open Policy Agent~\cite{opa2024} layer turns the mode, the kill-switch, and the live Trust Factor into an \texttt{allow}, \texttt{flag}, or \texttt{deny} decision under three modes: shadow (observe), advisory (alert), and enforcement (block).
Every request, signal, and decision becomes a signed, append-only audit record, and a scheduled canary feeds synthetic traffic so the signal continues without organic load.
The runtime keeps the framework simple through graceful degradation: the bus (Kafka/Redpanda~\cite{kreps2011kafka,redpanda2024}), the audit index (OpenSearch~\cite{opensearch2024}), and the policy layer each fall back to a file or in-process equivalent when absent.
The lite profile instantiates none of it; the same code, opted in via a Docker profile, becomes the full runtime.

\subsection{Governance Artifact Generation}
Model cards follow the Model Cards format~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard}: twelve measurable rows, dataset evaluation URIs, benchmark provenance, signatures, and limitations.
Datasheets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets} attach to corpus scans.
Environmental hooks record per-run energy and emissions for N03.

\subsection{Implementation and Deployment Profiles}
The \texttt{vera} package targets Python~3.11 and ships three deployment profiles over one code base: a single-command \emph{lite} stack (Redis, API, worker, and a Next.js dashboard, local artifacts, no login); a full \emph{enterprise} Docker Compose stack (adding Keycloak RBAC, MLflow, MinIO, TimescaleDB); and a \emph{gaas} overlay that adds the governance runtime of \Cref{sec:gaas}.
Every layer degrades gracefully: absent MinIO or MLflow fall back to local artifacts and no tracking, and an absent broker, policy server, or audit index fall back as in \Cref{fig:gaas}, so partial deployments still run.
The dashboard front-end (\Cref{sec:dashboard}) consumes a dedicated read API and an admin control plane.
The platform is validated by 99 backend unit tests and a 35-case Playwright matrix (role-based access, the guided surfaces, the governance panel, and the language toggle); the optional \texttt{VERA\_E2E\_OLLAMA} path replicates a full run.

\section{The VERA Control Room}
\label{sec:dashboard}
The dashboard is contribution C1: it turns the requirement scores into a view a non-specialist can read, following ``overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand''~\cite{shneiderman1996eyes}.

\paragraph{Status-first triage}
The requirement table is sorted by state (failed, fallback, uncovered, ok, n/a) rather than by identifier, so the weakest evidence surfaces first, and passing rows collapse behind a ``show all'' control.
Scores are shown as green, amber, and red bands, not a binary pass or fail, because release decisions are human trade-offs rather than threshold cliffs.

\paragraph{Explaining a score}
This is contribution C2.
Each row shows a score, an interval, and a one-line rationale.
Clicking a row opens a drawer with the contributing public benchmarks, the model card, and a sample output, so a reader sees \emph{why} a requirement is weak, not only that it is.
A run inspector exposes the run file, the git SHA, the signature, and the per-benchmark provenance.

\paragraph{Charts}
A trend line is drawn only once at least two runs of the same model exist; otherwise the dashboard shows a single coverage band.
It does not fabricate a time series from a single point.

\paragraph{Role-based views and a guided mode}
Three views (responsible-AI, cyber, and data-science) filter the requirement set per role, with an executive view that hides internal detail.
A no-login guided mode is the default: a four-step wizard (pick a connected model, choose a recommended or custom requirement set, set options, review) submits a run and opens a live summary.
This is the path a non-technical user takes, while the role-based views are the expert surface (\Cref{fig:dashboard}; more screens in \Cref{app:dashboard}).

\section{Evaluation Setup}
\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{Research Questions}
We ask three questions, one per contribution, following explicit-RQ empirical SE work~\cite{liu2022mscpdp,weninger2018usecase}.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{RQ1 (usable dashboard).} Can a non-specialist drive a run and read its results from the dashboard alone, without writing code? (C1)
  \item \emph{RQ2 (explainable scores).} Does VERA show \emph{why} a requirement is weak, by decomposing the score across criteria and the public benchmarks behind it? (C2)
  \item \emph{RQ3 (modular coverage).} Does one framework evaluate the twelve requirements across different model families and sizes, and adapt to a different specification without code changes? (C3, C4)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Subject Systems and Procedure}
We run two panels of self-hosted open-weight models, so the same framework is exercised across brands and across sizes.
Panel~A holds three families at a comparable size: Llama~3.1~8B, Qwen2.5~7B, and Gemma~2~9B.
Panel~B holds one family across three sizes: Ministral~3B, Mistral~7B, and Mistral-Small~24B.
A Llama~3.1~8B judge scores the refusal and jailbreak probes.
We evaluate all twelve measurable requirements CR01--CR12 with seed~42 and $n{=}10$ items per benchmark.
The dataset-stage requirements CR03--CR05 run over the synthetic corpus of \Cref{sec:generated}, so they are identical across models.
\Cref{tab:datasets} lists the public dataset behind each benchmark.

\emph{Serving note.} Ollama's chat endpoint exposes no token log-probabilities, so the log-likelihood tasks (CR06 capability, CR10 bias) fall back to VERA's dynamic probes, recorded in provenance.
The security probe \texttt{decodingtrust\_adv} likewise falls back on Apple Silicon.
The CR03--CR05 and CR12 engines are fully native on any backend.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We report a run of both panels (seed~42, $n{=}10$).
The numbers illustrate reporting and cross-model contrast, not state-of-the-art capability.

\subsection{RQ1: A Non-Specialist Can Read a Run}
We assess the dashboard as a design validation: two of us drove a run in guided (no-login) mode and read it from the dashboard alone (\Cref{fig:dashboard}; \Cref{app:dashboard}).
The status-first ordering puts the weakest requirement at the top, with passing ones collapsed behind ``show all''.
The coverage band, the Trust Factor, and the per-benchmark provenance are visible without scrolling, and ``fallback'' badges mark the degraded engines.
In one or two clicks each, the dashboard supported eight reading tasks: find the weakest requirement, read its band and interval, see which benchmarks fell back, judge coverage, reach a sample output, tell failed from fallback from ok, and launch a new run without the command line.
The alternative, correlating the run file and the per-item outputs by hand, takes minutes.
We report this as evidence that the interface \emph{affords} these tasks; a formal external panel~\cite{hayes2007krippendorff,studtmann2023histree} is future work.

\subsection{RQ2: Every Score Is Explained}
VERA does not stop at a number.
Opening a weak requirement shows its contributing public benchmarks and their individual scores, so the cause is visible rather than inferred.
For example, when a model scores low on representation bias (CR10), the drawer shows which bias benchmark drove it (BBQ, BOLD, or StereoSet), and clicking through reaches the sample outputs.
Each requirement score is therefore explained by the public evidence behind it, which is what RQ2 asks for.

\subsection{RQ3: One Framework Across Families and Sizes}
\Cref{tab:panelA} (families) and \Cref{tab:panelB} (sizes) report the twelve requirements for both panels, computed by the \emph{same} framework with no code change between them.
This is the benchmark contribution C4, and running it unchanged over two panels is the evidence for the modularity claim C3.
\Cref{fig:results-path} shows how to read a column: pick a requirement, read its band, and open it in the dashboard for the benchmark behind it.
The panels \emph{discriminate}.
On the family panel, fairness (CR11) splits the models sharply: Qwen2.5 collapses to $0.00$ while Llama and Gemma reach $1.00$, and cyber resilience (CR02) and toxicity (CR12) separate them too.
On the size panel, capability (CR06) rises with scale, from $0.70$ at 3B and 7B to $1.00$ at 24B, yet the 24B model collapses on fairness (CR11 $=0.00$) and cyber resilience (CR02 $=0.00$): a bigger model is not automatically a safer one.
These are exactly the cross-model gaps a reader must catch.
At $n{=}10$ items per benchmark, extreme splits such as CR11 $0.00$ versus $1.00$ are flags to investigate, not precise estimates; bootstrap intervals ship with the run artifacts.
The dataset-stage scores CR03--CR05 are identical across models, since the scans run on the corpus, not the model: CR03 $=0.84$, CR04 $=0.94$, and CR05 $=0.73$ (Presidio flags the planted PII).
Watermarking CR09 is $0.00$ for every model: the statistical test~\cite{kirchenbauer2023watermark} looks for a Kirchenbauer-style green-list bias, and open models carry no such watermark, so the correct score is zero rather than a false positive.

\subsection{Governance Runtime Performance}
\label{sec:gaas-eval}
To check that the governance runtime of \Cref{sec:gaas} is a working system, not only an architecture, we benchmarked its three load-bearing properties in-process against the real components (proxy, four agents, OPA layer, event bus).
\emph{Overhead.} The inline proxy adds a median $5.7$\,ms (p95 $8.2$\,ms) per request, about $2\%$ on top of a $280$\,ms local generation, so the synchronous gate is cheap relative to inference.
\emph{Detection.} On three planted abuse cases the agents fire correctly ($3/3$): the cyber agent flags a prompt-exfiltration jailbreak, the privacy agent flags a leaked synthetic IBAN, e-mail, and phone (Presidio), and the ethics agent flags an insulting reply (Detoxify), each below the $0.70$ trust threshold.
\emph{Enforcement and degradation.} The OPA layer denies under enforcement mode with the kill-switch engaged and allows a high-trust request under shadow; with no Kafka/Redpanda broker present, the event bus degraded to the Redis-Streams fallback and round-tripped a probe end-to-end, confirming the graceful-degradation paths of \Cref{fig:gaas}.

\subsection{Limitations}
Our evidence is a design validation, not a controlled user study; the dashboard walkthrough was performed by the authors, and a judge model (Llama~3.1~8B) may bias the LLM-judged probes.
CR06 and CR10 use dynamic probes under the Ollama log-probability constraint rather than native log-likelihood scoring; this is recorded in provenance, and a vLLM backend would let the native path corroborate those scores.
The panels cover open-weight models from 3B to 24B at $n{=}10$ items per benchmark, so larger models or more items may shift the numbers; the reported scores illustrate the framework's reporting, not a capability leaderboard.
The COMPL-AI scores are technical proxies for regulatory obligations~\cite{guldimann2024complai,euaiact2024}, not legal conclusions, and the bands are aids for human judgement, not automatic gates.
The governance-runtime benchmark measures per-request overhead and detection on planted cases, not sustained production load.

\section{Generated Data and a Banking Application}
\label{sec:application}

\subsection{Generated Data}
\label{sec:generated}
The dataset-stage requirements CR03--CR05 run on a fully synthetic corpus that we generate and release, so no customer data is involved.
A seeded script produces 230 short banking documents (FR/EN retail, corporate, wealth, and complaint records) that plant the failure modes the data stage should catch: synthetic IBANs (\texttt{FR76} plus 23 digits), RIBs, e-mails, and phones for privacy; near-duplicate pairs for copyright; and imbalanced client segments for data adequacy.
The native engines then score privacy CR05 $=0.73$ (Presidio recall on the planted PII), copyright CR04 $=0.94$ (Levenshtein and BLEU near-duplicate detection), and data adequacy CR03 $=0.84$ (group-balance heuristics).
Because the scans run on the corpus, not the model, these scores are model-independent, and the generator makes them fully reproducible.

\subsection{Application in Banking}
VERA is generic: the models it evaluates are general-purpose, not banking-specific, and so is the framework.
It applies cleanly to a regulated setting, however.
Consider a release gate for a retail-banking assistant (account FAQs, product explanations, complaint triage).
Such a system is regulated both by the AI Act and, inside a financial institution, by sector rules a second line of defence already tracks.
The requirements VERA measures line up with obligations under DORA~\cite{dora2022}, the EBA ICT guidelines~\cite{eba2021ictrisk}, and the ACPR's work on AI in finance~\cite{acpr2020ai}; \Cref{app:banking} gives the detailed mapping.
The same run therefore yields both an AI-Act evidence pack and artifacts a banking reviewer recognises.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Responsible-AI metrics exist but sit in scattered research code, and a single global score hides why a model fails.
VERA is an open-source framework that implements the twelve measurable COMPL-AI requirements, explains each score by decomposing it across criteria and the public benchmarks behind it, and stays modular so it adapts beyond COMPL-AI.
We showed that a non-specialist can read a run from the dashboard alone (RQ1), that each verdict is explained by the public evidence behind it (RQ2), and that one framework covers the twelve requirements across model families and sizes with no code change (RQ3).
Across both panels VERA flags concrete responsible-AI gaps, and it exposes a watermarking requirement that no open model meets.
We release VERA as open-source software to speed up adoption and disseminate responsible-AI best practice.
Future work includes a formal practitioner study with inter-rater reliability~\cite{hayes2007krippendorff,studtmann2023histree}, native log-probability runs on a vLLM backend, and the role-based, per-persona scoring that this software-focused paper only sketches.
