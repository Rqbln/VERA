\begin{abstract}
Every deployment of a large language model in a bank ends at a signature.
The person who signs owns the risk.
They work in compliance or in risk management, and they do not write code.
The EU AI Act asks them for technical evidence on robustness, privacy, transparency, fairness and human oversight.
The suites that produce that evidence are command-line tools built for engineers.
They hand over raw result files, not a view a sign-off owner can act on.
We present VERA, an open-source, self-hostable dashboard built and deployed in the responsible-AI function of a large European bank.
This paper asks the question a tool description cannot answer: does the dashboard measurably change what a user gets done?
We report a within-subject study run by the platform itself.
Each participant answered six factual questions about a completed evaluation from its raw result files, then six matched questions on the dashboard.
The application measured the time per question and validated every answer server-side against a snapshotted key.
\ifstudydone
On ten paired sessions, correctness rose from 27 to 42 answers out of 60 with the dashboard; seven participants improved, three tied, none did worse (exact Wilcoxon p = .016).
Median time per question fell from 56 to 30 seconds, a difference short of significance at this sample size (p = .065).
\fi
The study module ships with the tool, so the measurement can be replayed on any deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A retail bank wants a conversational assistant in front of its customers.
Account questions, product explanations, complaint triage.
Before it ships, someone has to sign it off.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/fig1}
  \caption{Four moments, one path. Whatever the role, the user selects a served model and a requirement set, runs public benchmarks against it, reads the outcome through a role-filtered view in which each score can be opened, and exports the evidence tied to that run.}
  \label{fig:journey}
\end{figure*}

In our institution that person sits in the second line of defence, in compliance or in risk management.
They are accountable for the deployment.
They do not write code.
The question they must answer is not whether the model is good.
It is whether shipping it is defensible.
The EU AI Act~\cite{euaiact2024} sets the terms of that defence.
It asks for technical evidence across the trustworthy-AI principles: robustness, privacy, transparency, fairness, human oversight.
Evidence, not assurances.

The material to produce that evidence already exists.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} turns the Act's principles into eighteen technical requirements, twelve of which carry a measurable score.
HELM~\cite{liang2023helm} standardises which scenarios to report.
The lm-evaluation harness~\cite{gao2021lmevalharness} standardises how a benchmark is executed.
Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} probes jailbreaks; DecodingTrust~\cite{wang2023decodingtrust} assembles a trustworthiness suite.
Each solves one part of the measurement problem, and solves it well.
What none of them solves is the last step before a signature.
What they hand over is a directory of result files, and the person accountable for the decision cannot work from it.
We built VERA against that gap, inside the responsible-AI function of the bank, and \Cref{sec:background} details the three failures that motivated it.

A tool paper would stop there, describe the features, and let the reader take usefulness on faith.
This paper does not.
Software engineering in practice is a claim about effect, so we measured the effect.
We asked the only question that justifies building a dashboard at all.
With it, does a user get more right, faster, than from the files the suites already produce?

We answer with a within-subject study that the platform itself administers.
Each participant answers six factual questions about a completed evaluation using only its raw result files.
They then answer six matched questions using the dashboard.
Question sets swap between phases across participants, so content learning is neutralised.
The application measures time per question with a monotonic clock.
Every answer is validated server-side against a key snapshotted from the run, so correctness is a measurement, not a self-report.
Two research questions structure the study.
\textbf{RQ1 (quality):} do participants extract correct facts from an evaluation more often with the dashboard than from the raw artifacts?
\textbf{RQ2 (time):} do they do it faster?
A technology-acceptance questionnaire adds a secondary perception measure (RQ3).

The design has one more property worth stating up front.
The study is administered end to end by the platform.
Consent, profile, timing, validation and export are pages and endpoints of the product itself.
No facilitator sits with the participant, and no answer is graded by a human who knows the hypothesis.
The instrument ships with the tool, so the measurement can be repeated on any deployment, by anyone, including a sceptical reader.

VERA itself is not a prototype built for this paper.
It runs in the responsible-AI function of BNP Paribas, on self-hosted infrastructure, where release decisions for LLM-based applications are prepared.
The requirements it answers were collected there over a year.
The walls of \Cref{sec:background} were hit there, in real review meetings.
\Cref{fig:journey} shows what the tool asks of its user.
Pick a served model and a requirement set, run, read the outcome through the lens attached to your role, export the evidence.
No command line, no account, no configuration file on that path.

Our contribution is threefold.
First, \textbf{empirical evidence}: a paired quality-and-time comparison of dashboard reading against raw-artifact reading, with exact statistics suited to small industrial samples (\Cref{sec:study,sec:results}).
Second, \textbf{the tool}: an open-source, self-hostable dashboard (\Cref{sec:background,sec:vera}).
Its design answers six requirements traced to regulatory text, to the tool literature, and to a year of practice in the responsible-AI function of a large European bank.
Third, \textbf{a replayable instrument} (\Cref{sec:study}).
The study runs as a page of the product, with consent, timing, validation and export built in.
Any deployment can therefore reproduce the measurement.
VERA and the study module are available at \url{\repourl}.

\section{Background and Motivation}
\label{sec:background}

This section sets out what exists, why it stops short of the sign-off decision, and what practice demands instead.
The tool of \Cref{sec:vera} is the answer to those demands, and the study of \Cref{sec:study} measures whether the answer works.

\subsection{What exists}
\Cref{tab:related} positions VERA among the tools a practitioner would consider.
COMPL-AI~\cite{guldimann2024complai} is our closest reference point and the framework VERA builds on.
It is an independent effort by ETH Zurich, INSAIT and LatticeFlow AI.
It maps the Act's principles to eighteen technical requirements and benchmarks the twelve measurable ones.
It is not EU-funded and is not part of the Act's harmonised standards.
What it publishes is a study and a leaderboard.
VERA adopts its taxonomy and turns it into something an operator drives.
NIST and ISO frameworks~\cite{nist2023airmf,iso42001_2023} guide organisational risk management but stop short of runnable metrics.
HELM~\cite{liang2023helm}, lm-evaluation~\cite{gao2021lmevalharness}, Garak~\cite{derczynski2024garak} and DecodingTrust~\cite{wang2023decodingtrust} are measurement building blocks.
VERA reuses them through its runners rather than competing with them.
A separate software-engineering line applies NLP and LLM techniques to the regulation itself, extracting obligations and checking artifacts against them~\cite{abualhaija2025llmregextraction,amaral2022nlpcompliance,abualhaija2024regchange}.
That work stops where ours starts: we take a requirement taxonomy as given and make it operable.
PromptOps~\cite{sunetnanta2025promptops} shares our diagnosis that the missing interface holds back adoption, but scores prompt by prompt and reports a flat set of numbers.
Model Cards~\cite{mitchell2019modelcard} and Datasheets~\cite{gebru2021datasheets} are the community documentation formats; both are normally authored after the fact.
For the evaluation method we start from HISTREE~\cite{studtmann2023histree}, which pairs a requirements argument with an acceptance questionnaire~\cite{davis1989tam}.
We add the paired task comparison that neither reports.

\subsection{Why it stops short}
We hit three walls preparing release decisions with these suites.
The first is the interface.
Every suite is driven from a command line built for machine-learning engineers, and the person accountable for the decision does not use one.
So an engineer ran the evaluation, and the result reached the review meeting as a slide.
That is where the evidence stopped being evidence.
The second is opacity.
Results arrive as a table of numbers with no path back to what produced them.
A reviewer who asks \emph{why} a requirement scores badly gets no answer from the tool.
They correlate a run file with per-item outputs by hand, and it takes minutes per question.
The third is drift.
The documentation that accompanies a release is written after the fact.
By the time it is filed, it describes an evaluation that has moved on.
None of these three is a measurement problem.
All three are software problems.

\subsection{What practice demands}
\label{sec:requirements}
A year of preparing release decisions under internal-control obligations condensed those failures into six requirements.
We state the source of each one, because a requirements list without sources is only a feature list in disguise.
\textbf{R1}, from observed practice: a non-specialist must be able to run an evaluation and read its verdict without writing code.
PromptOps reaches the same conclusion for developers~\cite{sunetnanta2025promptops}.
\textbf{R2}, from practice and the literature: every verdict must decompose into the public tests that produced it, because a single aggregate hides why a model fails.
\textbf{R3}, from regulatory text: the Act fixes in law what the technical file of a high-risk system must contain (Art.~11, Annex~IV).
Evidence meant to feed that file must carry the identity of the run it came from, or it will drift.
\textbf{R4}, from observed practice: views must differ by role, because legal, responsible-AI and data-science reviewers need different subsets and different vocabulary.
\textbf{R5}, from regulatory dynamics: the requirement set and the benchmarks must be replaceable without touching the code, because taxonomies move faster than tools.
\textbf{R6}, from institutional constraint: the tool must be self-hostable with no proprietary cloud dependency, because prompts and outputs cannot transit through a third party.

\section{VERA}
\label{sec:vera}

VERA is a Python~3.11 backend and a TypeScript single-page dashboard.
Whoever drives it follows the four moments of \Cref{fig:journey}; only the lens at the reading step differs by role.
This section keeps to what the study of \Cref{sec:study} exercises; the repository documents the rest.

\subsection{Architecture}
\Cref{fig:architecture} follows a launch request until it becomes a scored, documented run.
Three surfaces can issue it: a command line, a REST endpoint, and a guided wizard that needs no login.
All three normalise the request into one declarative job on a Celery/Redis queue.
A worker resolves each requirement to its benchmarks through a registry.
It drives the runners against self-hosted models through a local router.
It then folds cached item scores into per-requirement scores with bootstrap intervals.
What leaves the worker is a bundle: run file, model card, per-item outputs, content signature.
A read-only API hands it to the dashboard.
Two choices are load-bearing for \Cref{sec:requirements}.
Inference never leaves the self-hosted router, which is R6.
The requirement-to-benchmark mapping and the aggregation weights sit in two versioned data files, a registry and a signed catalog, selected by configuration.
That is R5, and it is exercised, not asserted.
The unchanged pipeline ran end to end under an alternative, security-focused specification.
The stack ships as a single-command lite profile and an enterprise profile with access control and object storage.
Missing optional services degrade to local equivalents rather than stopping a run.

\subsection{Features}
Six features answer the six requirements; \Cref{tab:mapping} closes the mapping in both directions.
\textbf{F1, guided launch}: a four-step wizard with defensible defaults submits a run in four clicks, with no account and no configuration file (R1).
\textbf{F2, role-based views}: three lenses (responsible-AI, cyber, data science) each show the subset a role answers for, bound to access control in the enterprise profile; the interface switches between English and French (R4, and R1 by subtraction).
\textbf{F3, explainable verdicts}: the run summary of \Cref{fig:dashboard} opens on a gauge over band-aligned segments, then a table ordered failed, degraded, uncovered, healthy.
Expanding a row lists the public benchmarks behind the score, what each returned, and one real model answer.
An inspector exposes the run file, the revision and the signature.
Wherever a simpler check stood in for an absent engine, an explicit marker says so (R2).
\textbf{F4, the non-measurable path}: the six requirements no benchmark can score are routed instead of blanked, to a human review queue with named reviewers and a rubric, to in-run energy instrumentation, or to signed declarative forms pinned to the run (extends R2, feeds R3).
\textbf{F5, run-tied artifacts}: every run writes its own model card, datasheet and signed export; nothing is authored afterwards, so nothing drifts (R3).
These artifacts feed a provider's risk assessment and its Annex~IV file; they are inputs to that work, not a substitute for it.
\textbf{F6, swappable specification on a self-hosted stack}: the alternative specification above plus the deployment profiles cover R5 and R6.
Regression is guarded by 173 backend unit tests and 43 browser scenarios that drive the product as a user would, including the study module of \Cref{sec:study}.

\section{Empirical Study}
\label{sec:study}

The claim behind every feature of \Cref{sec:vera} is that a reader does better with the dashboard than with what the suites already produce.
That claim is testable, so we test it.

\subsection{Design}
The study is within-subject with two conditions.
In the \emph{baseline} condition, the participant answers factual questions about a completed evaluation using only its raw result files.
These are the files the pipeline actually writes: the run record, the harness log, and the per-item model outputs, displayed in the study page as plain files.
In the \emph{dashboard} condition, the participant answers matched questions using VERA.
Every participant takes the baseline first and the dashboard second.
We fixed the order deliberately.
It mirrors how the artifacts reach a reviewer today.
And it puts any residual learning benefit on the dashboard side alone, where it is visible and declared (\Cref{sec:threats}).

Content learning is the real confound, and the design neutralises it with two matched six-item sets.
The sets ask the same six question types about different named targets.
The server assigns which set lands in which phase, alternating by participant number, so each set appears in each condition equally often across the sample.
The six pairs cover the reading path end to end.
Rank a requirement by weakness.
Read the score and confidence interval of a named requirement.
Trace benchmark provenance, and count the coverage.
Count verdicts by band.
Reach one raw model output of a named benchmark.
Each question has an objectively correct answer, computable from the run snapshot, in both conditions.
The item text states the band thresholds where they are needed, so the baseline lacks presentation, never information.

\subsection{Instrumentation}
The platform administers the study itself at a \texttt{/study} URL: consent, profile, both phases, and the questionnaire are pages of the product.
Access is symmetric by construction.
In the baseline phase, the raw files sit in tabs on the question page.
In the dashboard phase, each question carries a link that opens the dashboard on the view holding the answer: the run summary, a named requirement's drawer, or the harness log.
Both conditions therefore start one click from the right material, and neither link ever selects an answer.
A monotonic client clock starts when the participant presses start and stops at submission; server-side timestamps form an independent sanity channel.
Every answer is validated server-side against a key snapshotted when the session opens, and correctness is never disclosed during a session.
Questions are capped at five minutes, accept one submission, and offer an explicit give-up.
After the second phase, one non-comparative task asks the participant to launch an evaluation through the wizard; it feeds the R1 claim and stays out of the comparison.
A technology-acceptance questionnaire~\cite{davis1989tam} closes the session: four statements on perceived usefulness, four on perceived ease of use, five-point Likert, reported as a secondary perception measure only.

\subsection{Participants and ethics}
We target at least twelve complete sessions from research, data-science, product and IT profiles of the institution, excluding the paper's authors.
No name, employer or free-text identity field is collected.
Participants are identified by a server-assigned code; the profile questions (role, AI/ML experience, EU AI Act familiarity, seniority) use closed lists.
Invitations are tracked outside the application, so we can report the number invited and the response rate without linking any response to a person.
Results are reported in aggregate only.

\subsection{Analysis}
RQ1 compares, per participant, the number of correct answers out of six in each condition.
RQ2 compares, per participant, the median time per question, where a give-up or timeout censors its pair for time and counts as incorrect for quality.
Both use the exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test, computed by enumeration, because normal approximations are not defensible at this sample size.
Per pair, we report the discordant counts and an exact McNemar test as descriptive evidence of where the dashboard helps.
The analysis script ships with the tool and emits the rows of the tables in \Cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{RQ1: correctness by condition}
Participants answered 27 of 60 questions correctly from the raw files and 42 of 60 on the dashboard (45\% versus 70\%).
Seven of the ten improved with the dashboard, three tied, and none did worse; the exact Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the paired counts gives p = .016.
\Cref{tab:pairs} breaks the effect down by question pair and \Cref{tab:paired} by participant.
The largest effect is pair 5: one participant of ten could count the verdict bands from the raw files, at a cost of 108 seconds, and nine of ten could on the dashboard in a fraction of that time (exact McNemar p = .008); the coloured bands make the classification visible instead of computable.
Pair 4 is the one reversal, 7/10 against 3/10 in favour of the raw files: counting executed benchmarks is easier in a log that lists them line by line than in a view that aggregates them per requirement.
We report it as measured; it shows the instrument does not mechanically favour the tool.
One session, P25, was completed in a median of 8 seconds per question on the raw files and 3 on the dashboard, with seven give-ups; it ties at 1/6, is flagged by the straight-lining check, and no pre-registered rule allows excluding it.
Removing it leaves the quality result unchanged (p = .016) and moves the time result from p = .065 to p = .074.
An audit note on pair 6 closes \Cref{sec:results}.

\subsection{RQ2: time by condition}
The median of the per-participant median times is 56 seconds per question on the raw files and 30 seconds on the dashboard.
Seven of ten participants were faster with the dashboard, one tied, two were slower; the exact Wilcoxon test gives p = .065, directionally consistent but short of significance at n = 10.
The per-participant values sit in \Cref{tab:paired}; P25's times measure give-ups, not reading.
The exceptions are informative: P21 tied at 50 seconds; P3, the weakest genuine performer, gained correctness (0 to 2) but no speed; P27 gained one correct answer for six extra seconds.
Conversely the two strongest baseline readers, P15 and P26, tied on quality at 5/6 and nearly halved their time, one from each arm of the set assignment.
Readers who succeed on raw files gain speed; readers who fail on them gain correctness.

\subsection{RQ3: acceptance (secondary)}

\section{Discussion: VERA in Practice}
\label{sec:discussion}

The study measures the reading effect; this section states, factually, how the tool sits in practice.
VERA runs inside the responsible-AI function of BNP Paribas, on infrastructure the institution controls.
Reviewers use the guided mode with no account.
The human-review queue for the non-measurable requirements opens automatically at the end of every run.
The artifacts of \Cref{sec:vera} are generated per run, not written afterwards.
The specification-swap path is not hypothetical.
The repository ships a second, security-focused specification, and the unchanged pipeline produced verdicts under it.
The catalog digest is pinned to the run.
The study itself is part of that practice.
Because the instrument is a page of the product, the deployment that collects the data is the deployment being evaluated.
Any reader can rerun the measurement on their own instance.
What this paper does not claim is equally factual.
We do not claim decision outcomes.
Whether a full evaluation changes what a decision maker chooses is a different study, with a different population, and it is in progress.
We do not claim industrial time savings beyond the per-question measurements reported here.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Construct}
Correctness on six reading questions is a proxy for evaluation-reading ability, not for decision quality.
The two sets are matched by question type, not proven equally hard; the arm alternation spreads any residual difficulty difference across both conditions.
The acceptance items are perceptions and are reported as secondary.

\paragraph{Internal}
Attrition weakened the set counterbalance: assignment alternated evenly, but eight of the ten completers carried the same arm, so set and condition remain largely confounded in the analysed sample; the strongest-reader pattern replicating in both arms mitigates, without removing, this concern.
The matched-by-construction argument, not the counterbalance, carries the set-difficulty control at this n.
The condition order is fixed, baseline first.
Any residual learning therefore inflates the dashboard, which we accept and declare rather than counterbalance, and the matched sets remove the content component of that learning.
Nothing technically prevents a participant from opening the dashboard in another tab during the baseline phase; the page shows no link to it and the instructions forbid it.
Timing uses a client-side monotonic clock, which a mid-question reload restarts; the server timestamps reveal such cases.

\paragraph{External}
The realised panel is narrower than targeted: nine of ten completers are AI researchers of one institution, most with under two years of experience, not the compliance and risk owners the tool ultimately targets.
For the reading tasks this population is, if anything, adversarial to the hypothesis, since raw benchmark files are their native format; a gap in their favour of the dashboard is unlikely to shrink in a less technical population.
The study exercises one preloaded run on one model family.
Results speak to whether the dashboard changes what a reader extracts, not to whether it changes a sign-off decision.

\paragraph{Conclusion}
The sample is small, so we use exact tests throughout and report every per-participant value.
Participation is voluntary and self-selected.
The instrument, the raw exports and the analysis script are released, so the inference from data to claim can be checked.

\section{Lessons Learned}
\label{sec:lessons}

Four lessons from building and deploying the tool inside a bank transfer beyond it.

\paragraph{L1: the barrier was the first click, not the chart}
Reviewers explored a tool they could reach in a browser and abandoned one that asked for credentials first, whatever it showed them afterwards.
For a governance tool, the cost of the first interaction dominates the quality of every later one.

\paragraph{L2: a specification that lives in code will lag the regulation}
When the taxonomy was hard-coded, every disputed weight cost a code change, a review and a release.
Externalising it into a signed registry and catalog turned disagreements about weighting into arguments about a file that can be diffed, pinned to a run, and audited.
Externalising a normative model is what makes the norm reviewable.

\paragraph{L3: silent degradation is worse than a missing number}
A degraded check once read as a real result in an internal review.
We now propagate a fallback flag from the runner to the row to the exported artifacts, and sort degraded rows near the top.
Provenance of a number is part of the number.

\paragraph{L4: instrument the tool, not the session}
Building the study into the product cost about a week and removed the facilitator, the transcription and the scheduling constraint.
It also imposes a discipline: the answer key is snapshotted from the run under study, so the deployment must be pinned and purged before the first participant.
We got that wrong once in rehearsal, which is how it entered the protocol.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Evaluation suites for the EU AI Act produce the right measurements and stop one step short of the decision they are meant to inform.
VERA takes that step, and this paper measures the step instead of asserting it.
A within-subject study, administered by the platform, compares reading the raw result files against reading the dashboard.
It measures quality and time, paired per participant, with exact statistics.
The tool, the requirements it answers, the instrument and the analysis are all released.

Two lines of work are in progress.
The first examines how much a requirement verdict depends on the aggregation policy behind it, which is a property of the scoring method rather than of the tool.
The second replaces reading tasks with decision tasks, measuring whether practitioners choose differently when they see a full evaluation, with a panel drawn from compliance and risk functions.
